Why this project

This is a portfolio project demonstrating an end-to-end geospatial machine learning pipeline - from raw satellite imagery through a Random Forest classifier to a live interactive map. It combines Google Earth Engine for large-scale data access, scikit-learn for model training, a FastAPI backend, and vanilla JS on the front end.

Why Lanzarote

Lanzarote's stark volcanic landscape produces strong spectral contrasts in Landsat imagery - black basalt, golden enarenado farmland, white urban sprawl and blue coastline each sit in a different part of the electromagnetic spectrum. The island is also a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve facing real pressure from three decades of rapid tourism development, making the land cover story genuinely interesting.

What the data shows

Select a year above to see the classified Landsat composite for that period. The classifier was trained on CORINE 2018 land cover labels and applied to imagery spanning 1990 to 2023. Each pixel represents a 30 x 30 m Landsat cell. Use the panels below to read what each class means and how the island's area breakdown has changed over 33 years.

What you are looking at

Urban / Built-up
Residential zones, hotels, roads and industrial areas concentrated along the tourism corridors from Arrecife south to Playa Blanca.
- km² in 2023
Agriculture
Fields using the traditional enarenado technique - seeds planted under a layer of volcanic ash (picon) that traps overnight dew and prevents evaporation, allowing crops in one of Europe's driest climates without irrigation.
- km² in 2023
Barren / Volcanic
Raw lava flows, malpais basalt desert and exposed rock that blanket the island's interior. The Timanfaya volcanic complex erupted as recently as 1730-36, leaving terrain that remains geologically young and largely unvegetated.
- km² in 2023
Water / Wetland
Coastal salt flats (Salinas de Janubio), freshwater reservoirs and ephemeral streams that appear only after rare rainfall events. Lanzarote receives around 140 mm of rain per year on average.
